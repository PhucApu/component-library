Ticks a person would have chosen

The step is always one, two or five times a power of ten — never 3.7 — and the top and bottom of the plot land on a labelled tick rather than on whatever the largest value happened to be. The ticks are counted from the floor rather than added up, because adding a step to itself thirty times is how an axis ends up labelled 0.30000000000000004.

Names too long for the bottom

When labels stop fitting they are thinned out — every second, every third — and the last one is always kept, because on a time axis that is the one a reader looks for first. They are never turned on their side: a rotated label is slower to read, and on a narrow screen the axis ends up taller than the plot it belongs to. Names this long are usually a sign the chart wants to be horizontal.

There is no second axis, and no way to ask for one

Two scales on one plot invent a correlation the data does not have: where the two lines cross depends entirely on how the second axis was aligned, and that choice belongs to whoever drew the chart rather than to the numbers. Two measures of different size are two charts — or one chart with both indexed to a common base at the first period, which puts them on one honest scale.
